# Writing laws: what holds, what is folklore, and when to break it

**Prose is a way of moving a precise idea into another person's head.** Everything below serves that. A rule that does not serve it is folklore, and folklore is what makes writing sound like every other writing.

These laws come from Steven Pinker, *The Sense of Style* (2014) and *The Language Instinct* (1994), read in full for this repo on 22 October 2026. Nothing is quoted at length; the ideas are restated here and the sources are credited in `CREDITS.md`. This skill sits beside `skills/writing/`: that one edits a sentence, this one says which law the sentence broke and whether the break was allowed.

## The four moves

Every sentence a human keeps is doing four things. Check them in this order.

### 1. Land it — the idea arrives whole

Language exists to "cause precise new combinations of ideas to arise in each other's minds". That is the whole job. If the reader's head does not now contain the fact you meant to put there, the sentence failed, however elegant it is.

The neurological consequence is sharp. A vocabulary that lacks a word for a fact makes the fact unthinkable, not merely unmentionable — Newspeak's design goal was to make heretical thoughts *literally* unthinkable, "at least so far as thought is dependent on words". The practical version: **if you cannot name the thing, you have not thought it yet.** A vague sentence does not hide a vague thought; it hides the fact that the thought never happened.

Ask: can the reader repeat this back in their own words, correctly, without reading it twice?

### 2. Cut it — nothing that is not load-bearing

Omit needless words. But the sharper version is a cost, not a slogan: the seconds you add to your own life come out of minutes in your reader's life. Every clause you keep is a small tax on someone who has somewhere else to be.

Cut in this order: **the adverb, then the redundant clause, then the abstract noun, then the sentence.** A word that can go without the reader losing anything goes. A word that is doing real work stays even if it is long.

Do not cut a word that carries the voice. Cutting is not sanding.

### 3. Stage it — you are the camera, the reader is the audience

The passive voice is not a defect. It is a **camera angle**. Active puts the actor in the spotlight; passive holds the shot on whoever is having something done to them; a nominalization drifts the camera off both. The subject of a sentence is where the reader's attention lands first, so choosing a subject is choosing whose story the reader is watching.

Badly chosen, it makes the reader "crane back and forth like a spectator at a tennis match". Chosen, it is the most economical tool in English for withholding an agent who does not matter, or holding attention on the affected party.

**The failure is not the passive. The failure is a writer who forgot he was staging an event.** Symptom: the reader has to imagine an effect with no cause in view. That is as hard to picture as a grin with no cat.

### 4. Hold it — one spotlight, given before new

Two things keep a reader with you.

**One spotlight.** The subjects of consecutive sentences tend to be the same actor, and that string keeps the reader's eyes on a protagonist who is moving the plot. When a second actor takes over, announce the switch before it happens.

**Given precedes new.** Each sentence puts first what the reader already has, then what is new. Front-loading something the reader has not met yet is the single most reliable way to lose them.

Consistency follows from the two. Same action, same word, same label, everywhere it appears. A page that calls the same thing four different names is four different things to the reader.

## The laws that hold

These are worth enforcing. Most are non-negotiable.

| # | Law | What it protects | If broken |
|---|---|---|---|
| L1 | **Name the actor.** The reader can see who did it, unless the actor genuinely does not matter. | The cause of an event, so the reader can picture it moving. | The reader imagines an effect with no cause. |
| L2 | **Keep the spotlight.** One actor carries a passage; switches are announced. | The reader's thread through the paragraph. | Attention ping-pongs between characters. |
| L3 | **Given precedes new.** Familiar information first, new information last. | Comprehension order. | The reader meets an idea before the thing it depends on. |
| L4 | **Spell it out on first use.** Expand abbreviations, gloss jargon, then give an example. | The reader who does not share the clubhouse. | The reader flips back to the legend, or gives up. |
| L5 | **Find the verb.** If a sentence is full of noun-shaped abstractions, the act is unnamed. | Causality and responsibility. | The sentence describes a state and hides who is doing what. |
| L6 | **Say the plain thing.** If it cannot be said plainly, it is not finished. | The idea itself. | A slogan is doing the work a sentence should do. |
| L7 | **Know real errors from folklore rules.** | Your authority. | You "fix" a deliberate choice, or leave a real error. |

**L7 in practice.** Some rules are about the language and are worth enforcing: `aggravate` does not mean *annoy*; a dangling participle changes who did what; `fewer` for countable, `less` for uncountable. These confuse or misstate. Everything else on the lists people keep are about *style*, and style is the author's business.

A dictionary is not a governing body. The editors "pay attention to the way people use language". There is no in charge. Which is exactly why a rule you cannot justify from the reader's side is not a rule.

## The rules that do not hold

These are the commandments that make good prose worse. They are not forbidden — they are **badly stated**.

| Myth | Why it is badly stated |
|---|---|
| "Never use the passive voice." | The passive has indispensable functions and a millennium of use. A skilled writer chooses it deliberately. Copy-editors who blue-pencil every passive are erasing a decision. |
| "Never use an uncommon or fancy word." | Showing off with words you barely understand makes you look pompous. A rare word that lands is not showing off; it is precision. |
| "Write with nouns and verbs, not adjectives and adverbs." | The rule is about *turgid* prose stuffed with Latinisms and flabby modifiers, not about the parts of speech. |
| "Avoid alliteration." | Alliteration is one of the hallmarks of lively prose. It fails when it is the only thing happening. |
| "Murder your darlings." | Advice of the grim school. Savoring good writing is how a writer's ear is trained; the alternative is boot camp. |
| "Correct English is a fixed standard, and everything else is decay." | There is no in charge. And the complaints that get voiced are rarely about clarity, grace or coherence — they are about belonging to a club. |

**The test for any rule you are about to apply:** does it make the reader's job easier or harder? If it does neither, it is a preference, and preferences live in `STYLE.md` where the owner can see them.

## When to break a law for impact

A break is legitimate when it is a **camera move**, not a wobble. Four conditions, all required:

1. **Local.** One sentence, one clause, one paragraph. A break that becomes a style is not a break, it is a habit, and it will read as slop.
2. **Paid off.** The reader understands the break within the same breath it happens. A puzzle that resolves next paragraph is a puzzle.
3. **Oriented first.** The reader knows where they are before the break happens. You cannot be clever with someone who is lost.
4. **Named.** You can say what the break does to the reader — slows them, startles them, makes them look up. If you cannot, it is decoration, and decoration is what this repo exists to remove.

### Never break here

| Place | Why |
|---|---|
| The first screen of a document | The reader is not oriented yet. Impact without orientation is confusion. |
| UI labels, buttons, error messages | The reader is scanning, not reading. They want the verb. |
| Safety, medical, legal, financial copy | Ambiguity costs more than any style gain. |
| When the reader is non-native or pressed for time | Load-bearing clarity beats every effect. |
| When you are covering a mistake | Do not style an error into an event. State it. |

### Break it here

| Place | How |
|---|---|
| The second sentence of a piece | Once the reader has the frame, you can jump the frame. |
| A contrastive pair | Two short clauses, balanced, landing on the sharper one. |
| A deliberate fragment | One sentence without a verb, in a paragraph of verbs. It reads as emphasis because everything around it is complete. |
| A rare, exact word | When the common word is wrong, not merely plainer. |
| Silence | A short paragraph after a long one. The pause is the emphasis. |

## The reviewer's three questions

When you find a sentence that breaks a law, do not correct it reflexively. Ask:

1. **Is this a real error or a choice?** (L7) If the author meant it and it works, it is not a finding.
2. **Which law did it break?** Name it. A finding without a law is a preference, and a preference belongs to the owner.
3. **Would the break still work if the reader were tired?** If the effect depends on the reader being fresh and attentive, it is decoration.

If all three pass — the author meant it, it breaks a law on purpose, and it survives a tired reader — **it stays**. Say so in the report. A review that only lists removals is a review that has learned nothing about the work.

## What this layer refuses

- **No banned-word list.** Words are not the defect. `unlock` in a product that unlocks a lock is a verb, not a tell.
- **No passivity percentage.** There is no verified basis for a number that says how passive a text is. Quote the sentence.
- **No automatic rewriting of a break.** A break that lands is the author's, not the scanner's.
- **No flattening.** A rule that would turn a strange, deliberate, correct sentence into a plain one is not applied here.
