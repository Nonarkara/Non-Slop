Prose is a way of moving a precise idea into another person's head. Every rule below serves that.

## The four moves

1. **Land it.** The idea arrives whole. If you cannot name the thing, you have not thought it yet. A vague sentence hides an unfinished thought.
2. **Cut it.** Nothing that is not load-bearing. The seconds you add to your own life come out of minutes in your reader's life. Cut the adverb, then the redundant clause, then the abstract noun, then the sentence. Do not cut a word that carries the voice.
3. **Stage it.** You are the camera. Active puts the actor in the spotlight; passive holds the shot on whoever is affected; a nominalization drifts off both. The subject of a sentence is where the reader's attention lands first.
4. **Hold it.** One spotlight per passage, switches announced. Given precedes new. The same action gets the same word everywhere.

## The laws that hold

| Law | If broken |
|---|---|
| **L1 Name the actor.** The reader can see who did it, unless the actor does not matter. | The reader imagines an effect with no cause. |
| **L2 Keep the spotlight.** One actor carries a passage. | Attention ping-pongs between characters. |
| **L3 Given precedes new.** Familiar first, new last. | The reader meets an idea before what it depends on. |
| **L4 Spell it out on first use.** Expand abbreviations, gloss jargon, then give an example. | The reader flips back, or gives up. |
| **L5 Find the verb.** Noun-shaped abstractions hide the act. | The sentence hides who is doing what. |
| **L6 Say the plain thing.** If it cannot be said plainly, it is not finished. | A slogan does the work a sentence should. |
| **L7 Know real errors from folklore rules.** | You "fix" a deliberate choice, or leave a real error. |

Real errors confuse or misstate — `aggravate` does not mean *annoy*; a dangling participle changes who did what. Everything else on the lists people keep is style, and style belongs to the owner.

## The rules that do not hold

Badly stated, not forbidden: "never use the passive voice" · "never use an uncommon word" · "write with nouns and verbs, not adjectives and adverbs" · "avoid alliteration" · "murder your darlings" · "correct English is a fixed standard".

**The test for any rule:** does it make the reader's job easier or harder? If neither, it is a preference, and preferences live in `STYLE.md`.

## Breaking a law for impact

All four, or it is decoration:

1. **Local.** One sentence or paragraph. A break that becomes a style is not a break.
2. **Paid off.** The reader gets it within the same breath.
3. **Oriented first.** You cannot be clever with someone who is lost.
4. **Named.** You can say what the break does to the reader. If you cannot, cut it.

**Never break here:** the first screen of a document · UI labels, buttons, error messages · safety, medical, legal, financial copy · for a non-native or time-pressed reader · to cover a mistake.

## The reviewer's three questions

1. Is this a real error or a choice?
2. Which law did it break? A finding without a law is a preference.
3. Would the break still work if the reader were tired?

If all three pass — the author meant it, it breaks a law on purpose, it survives a tired reader — **it stays**, and the review says so. A review that only lists removals has learned nothing.

## What this layer refuses

No banned-word list · no passivity percentage · no automatic rewriting of a deliberate break · no flattening a strange, correct sentence into a plain one.
